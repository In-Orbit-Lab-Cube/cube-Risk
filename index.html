This document combines public historical cases with an engineering FMEA for a representative 1.5U educational CubeSat. The historical database includes known anomalies, recoveries, suspected causes, and unknown-cause no-contact cases. The FMEA is prospective: it describes what could fail in your design and how to detect, recover from, or mitigate it.

Main design implication: prioritize the power path, radio/antenna/first-contact chain, onboard-computer boot and recovery logic, and fault containment. These functions are tightly coupled: a power fault can disable the radio; a radio fault can prevent recovery commands; a software loop can drain the battery or block deployment.
Evidence discipline: there is no publicly available, uniformly classified database that gives reliable occurrence rates for every individual failure mode across all CubeSats. Therefore, subsystem trends are based on published surveys; the ordering of individual FMEA modes is a screening judgment, not a claim that one exact component fails more often than another. Unknown root causes remain “unknown.”

1. Scope, assumptions, and scoring method

Scope

In-orbit failures and anomalies, including launch-induced latent damage that manifests after deployment. Domains: EPS, communications/TT&C, C&DH/flight software, ADCS, thermal control, structure/deployment, payload/data, and ground/operations.

Representative 1.5U assumptions

Actual components have not been supplied. Each mode is conditional on the architecture: reaction wheels, magnetorquers, heaters, deployable panels, and payload interfaces may not exist on your spacecraft. Mark not-applicable rows after the bill of materials and architecture are known.

Historical evidence categories

Documented: described by a mission team or published investigation. Probable: the team identifies a likely cause but not absolute proof. Unknown: a symptom/status is public but the root cause is not. Engineering mode: plausible FMEA candidate, not claimed as a historical event in the listed satellite.

RPN method

Severity (S), occurrence (O), and detectability (D) are scored 1–5. RPN = S × O × D. Higher D means harder to detect/isolate. The scores below are provisional engineering judgments—not statistically measured probabilities.

Rating guide

ScoreSeverity (S)Occurrence (O)Detectability (D)
1Negligible; no mission impactRare / strong controls and little relevant evidenceEasy to detect with direct telemetry
2Minor degradationUnlikelyUsually detected promptly
3Major function degraded; workaround possiblePlausible / known class of issueNeeds correlation or diagnostic tests
4Major mission objective at riskElevated concern / weak margin or limited evidenceDifficult to isolate among several causes
5Loss of spacecraft or missionFrequent or strongly supported for this exact design (do not use without evidence)Hard to detect before consequences; telemetry may be absent

Risk bands used in this report: RPN ≥ 60 Critical; 40–59 High; 20–39 Moderate; below 20 Lower. These bands are a project-screening convention, not a universal standard. Severe single-point failures should be reviewed even if their RPN is below a threshold.

3. What failure trends can—and cannot—be concluded

Published CubeSat reliability analyses repeatedly identify electrical power and communications as major subsystem-level failure categories. One published RAMS analysis reported approximately 40% of recorded failure cases as dead-on-arrival/no-contact and found that EPS and communications remained prominent causes; its dataset and classifications should not be treated as universal current probabilities for every CubeSat. See the linked CEAS Space Journal analysis in the references.

Another survey notes that early failures are often classified as unknown because no telemetry is available. This means a “no signal” event is a symptom, not a component-level root cause. The radio, power, boot software, antenna deployment, ground station, orbit/object identification, or more than one of these may be involved.

Evidence-supported trendWhat it means for your projectWhat not to claim
EPS and communications are prominent failure categories in published surveys.Prioritize power generation/storage/distribution and the complete TT&C path.Do not claim an exact per-component probability without a compatible dataset.
No-contact / dead-on-arrival cases are common in historical databases.Design for first contact, minimal health beacons, low-power recovery and good ground procedures.Do not label every silent spacecraft as a radio failure.
Root cause is easier to establish when telemetry and logs exist.Persist reset reasons, current/voltage/temperature data, mode changes and raw packets.Do not claim a suspected cause as confirmed.
Some failures are recovered through ground operations rather than hardware repair.Include distributed stations, command rehearsal, orbit-ID cross-checks and safe recovery commands.Do not assume an onboard fault when a ground-station fault can explain the observation.
Mechanisms can create hard-to-repair single points of failure.Minimize deployables and test them as integrated systems when they are mission-critical.Do not buy redundancy or mechanisms without a mission requirement and volume/power budget.

5. Risk priorities for an educational, low-cost 1.5U CubeSat

For a small educational mission, the objective should be to maximize mission resilience and diagnostic ability per unit cost, mass, volume, power, and team workload. The recommendations below favor independent recovery paths, fault containment, clear telemetry, and integrated testing before expensive full-unit redundancy.

Small spacecraft surveys identify EPS and communications as major failure categories; IDEASSat and CuPID provide concrete cases.Allocate design review and test effort first; build fault injection and end-to-end tests; instrument critical rails and reset paths.

P2Make the mission tolerant of payload failurePayload power/interface/softwareFor an educational mission, bus survival and a successful basic mission can be more valuable than saving the experiment at any cost.Separate payload power; define a bus-only safe mode; limit payload duty cycle; prioritize housekeeping and TT&C over payload downlink.
P3Add redundancy only where justifiedSecond radio, redundant OBC, duplicate sensors, extra deployment mechanismRedundancy adds mass, volume, cost, power and integration burden; in 1.5U it may create new failure modes and may be infeasible.Prefer functional independence, resetability, load isolation, software recovery and ground-station diversity before duplicating entire units. P3Manage thermal and ADCS risk according to actual architectureBattery thermal limits, attitude-dependent power, sensor/actuator setRisk is highly dependent on orbit, attitude-control method, panel configuration and payload dissipation; avoid buying complex hardware without a requirement.Use analysis and test first; prioritize passive thermal design and simple safe modes; if no reaction wheels or heaters are used, mark those modes not applicable rather than assigning them as actual components.

Recommended engineering allocation

  1. First priority — first contact and survival: validate battery state at deployment, startup sequencing, safe-mode power, radio configuration, antenna behavior, and ground-station pass/ID procedures.
  2. Second priority — avoid cascading failures: isolate payload and noncritical loads; bound software retries; ensure an OBC reset or recovery mechanism cannot depend entirely on the software that has failed.
  3. Third priority — diagnostic visibility: instrument critical power rails and battery, log reset causes and mode transitions, and preserve raw downlink packets.
  4. Fourth priority — integrated verification: test actual radio + antenna + ground station, actual power system + representative loads, and flight software + real peripherals. Unit tests alone do not verify interfaces.
  5. Fifth priority — only justified redundancy: use ground-station diversity and software recovery first when practical. Duplicate hardware only where the mission benefit outweighs added integration complexity and resource cost.
Key educational-project rule: a test that reveals a design weakness before launch is a successful verification outcome. Do not remove essential verification simply to save cost; reduce complexity, scope, and mechanisms before removing the tests that verify mission-critical functions.

Before freezing the risk register

  • Replace generic components with selected part numbers and interfaces from your actual 1.5U architecture.
  • Mark each row as applicable, not applicable, or unresolved. Do not score hardware that is not part of the design.
  • Revise occurrence scores using mission-specific evidence, heritage, component qualification, environmental assumptions, and test results.
  • Record the rationale for each severity and detectability score; distinguish detectability of the symptom from isolation of the root cause.
  • Recalculate residual RPN after mitigations, but do not close a risk solely because the number decreased. Verify that the mitigation has been tested.

8. Limitations and next engineering step

  • This is a literature-based preliminary analysis. It is not an official reliability prediction, FMEA approval, safety assessment, or flight-readiness review.
  • RPN values are initial judgments. They must be updated with your real design, orbit, component choices, mission duration, and verification results.
  • Historical mission sizes and dates are given as reported in the cited sources. Exact construction completion dates are often not public, so launch year is not represented as build year.
  • The “frequency” ordering for individual failure modes is not statistically established. Published sources more reliably support broad subsystem trends than exact component-level rates.
  • For a 1.5U design, some 1U/2U/3U/6U historical cases may differ substantially in antenna, power, ADCS, thermal, and payload architecture.

Recommended next step: map this generic FMEA to your system architecture and bill of materials, then create a traceable risk register with requirements, interfaces, verification evidence, responsible owner, and residual risk.
